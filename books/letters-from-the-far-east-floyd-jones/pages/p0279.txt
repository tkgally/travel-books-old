This being one of my first experiences in a tea house, I felt somewhat concerned as to how I should fare in the way of a bed; but my guide having taken the precaution of bringing along a pair of sheets, I found, on entering the sleeping apartment, a most comfortable one on the floor—made up of a number of Japanese comforters, which are heavily wadded, and make a very soft bed. There are no bedsteads in Japanese houses—hence the necessity of sleeping on the floor. As an additional precaution, the sheets were sprinkled with flea-powder, but I'm fully of the opinion that this was unnecessary, nor did I find any in the houses that I afterwards occupied; but I'm told, on good authority, that in midsummer they are abundant and very annoying.

Having a busy day before us, we were aroused quite early, and, taking a good hearty breakfast, set out to inspect the famous shrines and tombs, which were chiefly the objects of our visit. Almost the first interesting object that one meets on leaving the tea house is the sacred bridge, called in Japanese Mihashi.
