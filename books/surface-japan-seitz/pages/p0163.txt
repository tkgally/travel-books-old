The Japanese houses are without plaster ceilings, but have tile roofs set in mud on a thin sheathing. The mud dries out, and commonly in a shake the roof slides off. The custom of binding the tiles has not yet arrived from across the sea. In the foreign-built house the chimneys are apt to disintegrate. They do not always fall, but slew around in sections, perilously shattered and apparently ready to drop at a breath. With customary optimism, the earthquake is regarded as a weather-maker. A shake is expected to produce a change.

One thing comes readily to the mind in Japan. The people are tired of tutelage and of being “interpreted” and “understood.” They are plain folk like most of us, and seek direct contact without the help of middlemen, whether they be commission merchants, missionaries, consuls, or ambassadors. They conceal their annoyance, but are no less annoyed at an insistence of assistance where they feel it is no longer needed.
