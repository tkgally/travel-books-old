But as less than seven hundred thousand souls are employed in factories the figures are more or less misleading in comparison with the much greater factory employment in the other countries named, while the industries are much less diversified, being mainly cotton spinning and weaving.

These figures show that but few of Japan’s fifty million have been caught and caged by the manufacturer and set to grinding in the treadmill of fixed hours inside of factory doors. Those who have been toil long in a double shift from nine in the morning until nine at night, and from the latter hour until the morning nine comes again. So strikes are frequent—not so much, it might be surmised, over wages as from weariness at the irksomeness of the toil. For in all Japan the workman works as he likes in his own house, turning exquisite woodwork on his foot-driven lathe, carving elegantly, setting his marvelous inlays or making silver, bronze, or gold obey his will.
