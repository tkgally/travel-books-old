A breech-clout, a paper umbrella, and a cigarette form the popular summer costume in rural and suburban Japan, where, by the way, they have a saying, “from breech-clout to breech-clout, from great-grandfather to great-grandson,” which is a good equivalent for our own “from shirt-sleeves to shirt-sleeves in three generations.”

The Butterfly of Hokusai which Whistler borrowed and made his own was the moth of the silkworm, the most valuable resource known to Japan. The lustrous fiber which wraps it before birth forms the finest fabric worn by women, and nowhere do the weavers work the woof more deftly. The rattle of the Jacquard loom may not be heard in Japan, but all about Kyoto the distaffs turn the gossamer into thread, and this in time the hand-looms make into cloths so delicate that they seem to be fashioned out of mist.
