The garden is picturesque, with a charming lake in the centre, bordered with pine trees, and a little pine grove island. The lake is stocked with carp, which are accustomed to being fed by visitors, and will come in crowds at the clapping of hands to where the pieces of cake or cracker are thrown. These fish are looked upon as sacred, and are never caught or killed.

The “golden pavilion,” which once shone with dazzling glory, is dull and worn, bereft of all its gold. The apartments contain folding and sliding screens, and kakemonos by the most celebrated Japanese artists, besides relics and autographs of the Shōguns and other celebrities.

The temple of Chion-in stands upon a hill in eastern Kyoto. A broad avenue lined with cherry trees leads up to it. This temple was founded in 1211 <sc>a. d.</sc>, by Enko-Daishi, who developed a new doctrine, called the road to the “Pure Land,” from which a sect, known as Jodo, meaning pure land, was named. Chion-in is the principal monastery of this sect. These temples are universally simple and imposing, impressing one with the solemnity of his surroundings, and, like all the others, this one contains many objects of interest within its walls.
