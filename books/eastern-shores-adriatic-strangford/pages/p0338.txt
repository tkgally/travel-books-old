To the eye of the Turk and the conservative diplomatist, who stand on antique ways, to the tourist and the trader, the Bulgarian is merely a Greek Christian like another, only with a vernacular <i>patois</i> of his own; he is one of the ‘<i>Rum Milleti</i>’ or ‘Greek nation’ spiritually and intra-nationally administered by the Patriarchate, and nothing more. Yet it is antipathy to that Greek spiritual administration which has called his sense of nationality into existence, and which is as the very breath of its life. He insists on having bishops and clergy of his own race and speech; he will not tolerate an alien priesthood, who are too often both the originators and the instruments of oppression and tyranny; he seeks to obtain the established use of his language as an instrument of prayer and education; and, rather than be deprived of this, he will go over to the Church of Rome.
