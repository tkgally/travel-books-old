Some of these gardens are of exquisite design and beauty and must cost infinite labour to lay out and maintain; yet behind paltry little sheds, that no self-respecting English workman would accept as a dwelling, these gardens lie hidden from the casual passer-by—an impressive and ubiquitous testimony to the innate æsthetic sense and artistic genius of the Japanese populace.

The population of Nikko, who produce and tend these beautiful gardens, are highwaymen of a polite and affable type, who lurk in ambush throughout the day, darting out upon the approach of pilgrims to lure them to their stalls.

But after we had spent three days in Nikko, the brigands had become cured of this predatory propensity; for our party included, as I have mentioned, a certain brisk, sharp-tongued little Cornish lady of my near acquaintance, whose method of dealing with the raiders proved curiously dissuasive and devastating.

She never declined their invitations. She never refused to look at anything. She complacently inspected and rummaged all the treasures that were shown to her and serenely listened to all the pirates’ praise of their artistic worth.
