We learned further that “there is a small maggot which appears to infest all Chinese prisons; the earth at the depth of a few inches swarms with them; they are the scourge most dreaded by the poor prisoners. Few enter a Chinese gaol who have not on their bodies or limbs some wounds, either inflicted by blows to which they have been subjected or caused by the manner in which they have been bound. Bound and helpless, the poor prisoner cannot save himself from the maggots’ approach, and if they once succeed in reaching his lacerated skin, there is the certainty of a fearful, lingering, and agonising death before him.”

I had read aforetime that in the provincial gaols the condition of the wretched culprits is even worse, and that after the taking of Canton by our troops in 1859, prisoners were dragged out of one prison who had been lying by the side of a corpse in an advanced state of decomposition, in a foul, dark den, so pestilential that our soldiers only found it possible to endure it for a moment.
