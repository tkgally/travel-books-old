Nor did the gallant Captain pause to ask me whether I was English or American. It seemed sufficient for him that a white man in Africa was in need of assistance. On the following day he kindly gave me a passage to Zanzibar.

My dispatches were sent and letters written. Congratulations, that I had prevailed against the <i>Herald</i>-German combination, and revenged myself by gaining a complete victory were cabled by the managers of <i>The World</i>, from New York.

A fortnight’s stay in Egypt, <i>en route</i> homeward, and a brief stay in London were made. Special courtesies were shown me at the Savage and Whitehall Clubs, London, and by the Hon. Secretary of the Royal Geographical Society, who invited me to write a paper, to be read before the Society. By the end of February, 1890, I was again in New York. I had been gone fourteen months. I had not “found Stanley,” as Stanley had found Livingstone in 1871; the circumstances were altogether different. I had, however, gratified a pardonable journalistic ambition in being the first correspondent to reach him and to give him the news of the world, after his long period of African darkness. That I had done this under most trying conditions, Mr. Stanley fully appreciated; and warmly reciprocated by showing me every courtesy in his power, on the march to the coast, in Zanzibar, and in Egypt.
