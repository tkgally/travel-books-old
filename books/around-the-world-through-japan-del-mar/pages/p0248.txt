We were served on the little ceremonial lacquer tables (<i>zen</i>) about ten inches high, one small boy keeping our bowls filled with boiled rice (<i>meshi</i>) from the rice-tub (<i>ohachi</i>), another brought in the various courses in covered lacquer bowls, while a third replenished our cups with warm <i>sake</i> (<i>kanzake</i>).

<i>Sake</i>, the national drink, is brewed from rice with scarcely any other ingredient but water. The malt (<i>kōji</i>) is made from husked but uncleaned rice; the yeast (<i>moto</i>) is formed from <i>kōji</i> and mixed with steamed rice-and-water, and heated to induce alcoholic fermentation. More steamed rice-and-water mixed with proper proportions of <i>kōji</i> and <i>moto</i> are fermented and then filtered. The <i>sake</i> is thus obtained, and has an alcoholic strength of about eleven per cent. It is difficult to preserve in warm weather, and is therefore mostly brewed in winter, and the whole brew disposed of within a twelve-month. It has a flavour of light sherry or marsala, and is sipped warm or cold.

Each table held four bowls (<i>hira</i>), one for the rice and the other three containing soups (<i>shiru</i>) or stewed vegetables of sorts. Three times the tables were changed, and fresh dishes presented, until we had had a full dozen different messes placed before us, all of which we tasted, even to the evil-smelling <i>diakon</i> and <i>kōnomono</i> (radishes pickled or preserved in salt and bran). There was soup (<i>misoshiru</i>) seasoned with <i>miso</i> (a sauce made of beans, wheat, and salt); there was “the honourable soup” (<i>o shiru</i>), and still another soup of bean-curds (<i>suimono</i>); there was a dish of vegetables called <i>choku</i>, and a mysterious mixture known as <i>howait;</i> there was buckwheat (<i>soba</i>) flavoured with soy (<i>shōyū</i>, a sauce made of fermented beans and wheat); there was pickled seaweed (<i>sushi</i>), and a red bean sauce with sugar (<i>shiruko</i>), both eaten with rice cakes; and there was, at the finish, dessert (<i>kuchitori</i>), consisting of sponge cake (<i>kasutera</i>) and a sweet paste made of beans (<i>yōkan</i>), which were the only things we tasted twice.
