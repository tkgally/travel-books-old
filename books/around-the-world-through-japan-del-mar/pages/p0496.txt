On the eve of our departure our friends gave us an evening’s entertainment, beginning with a dinner which was noteworthy, not only for its cheery hospitality, but also for the exceptionally large mushrooms with which we were served, and a most delicious <i>entremets</i>, in the shape of roast bananas. The recipe is simple. Place your bananas, short and fat ones for choice, in an oven for fifteen to twenty minutes, and serve hot in their jackets. When you have eaten these, plain or with a little butter, you will be prepared to believe that you have hit upon the real ambrosia, the food of the gods. Our after-dinner coffee was grown in the Kona district of the island of Hawaii, and the flavour is equal, if not superior, to the best coffee we could get in Java. After dinner we were taken to a show at the “Orpheum” and later to a <i>luau</i>—one of the great institutions of the country.

In its native simplicity the <i>luau</i> is an open-air picnic, where the ground, covered with a few fresh leaves, forms the table; where flowers are the most conspicuous articles of attire worn by the dusky beauties; where fish cooked in <i>ti</i> leaves, a sort of sea-weed called <i>limu</i>, and calabashes of <i>poi</i> are eaten with the fingers; and where friends, acquaintances, and even strangers are welcome to join the party and partake of a generous hospitality. But our <i>luau</i>, given by a native lady, partook more of the nature of a cold supper, where the guests sat down in a spacious dining-room literally smothered in choice flowers, the very chairs being twined with <i>leis</i> and the table one mass of them. The fish, the <i>limu</i>, and the <i>poi</i> were on the table, but knives and forks took the place of fingers, except for the <i>poi</i>, with which the natives would no more use a knife, fork, or spoon than an Englishman would to eat asparagus. An excellent string band played native airs, and after supper some of the guests danced the <i>hula</i> and then the cake-walk, an importation from the music-halls of San Francisco; and at 2 <sc>a.m.</sc> the popular and catching song of farewell, “<i>Oloha Oe</i>,” rose in full chorus, and we had finished our last evening in Honolulu.
