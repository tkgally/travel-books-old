Some coolies wear <i>tabi</i> with <i>hechima</i> fibre soles, which are tough enough to dispense with the protecting <i>geta</i>. This fibre is from the interior of the snake-gourd; and this interior, taken out in one piece, is the ordinary loofah (or luffa) which finds its place in European bathrooms. At some inns you will be given a pair of straw sandals with rope soles (<i>zori</i>); but as a rule you walk in stocking feet over the bare boards of the platform to your room. The passageways are three feet wide, no more, no less.

The floors of the rooms are sunk about three inches, so as to bring the top of the mats (<i>tatami</i>) on a level with the passages, and as each mat measures three feet by six feet, the rooms always measure some multiple of three feet each way. On one side of the room is a little alcove (<i>tokonoma</i> or <i>toko</i>), three feet or less in depth, and this is intended to contain the hanging scroll (<i>tatsumono</i>) or hanging picture (<i>kakemono</i>), and a shelf for the flower-vase (<i>hana-ike</i>), the incense-burner (<i>koro</i>), and the family shrine (<i>butsudan</i>), containing the funeral tablets (<i>ihai</i>). Otherwise there is no furniture nor any ornaments, except, perhaps, a lacquered box for writing materials. The ornaments placed on the <i>toko</i> are known collectively as <i>okimono</i>, and similarly the hanging scrolls and pictures are <i>kakeji</i>, while pictures and scrolls that are not intended to be hung up are called <i>makimono</i>. On fête-days the <i>toko</i> in a Buddhist household will contain three <i>kakemono</i>, one of a Buddhist deity, and the other two being pictures of flowers, birds, or animals, varying according to the season.
