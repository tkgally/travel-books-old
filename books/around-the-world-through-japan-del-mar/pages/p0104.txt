# Chapter VII

[[subtitle]] Singapore

[[subtitle]] Sir Thomas Raffles. The Chinese Paradise. The Straits Settlements. The Malay Language. First Impressions. Club Temperance. Fruit. Johor. Sampans. Tin Hill. Fast Coaling. Money. Justice. Servants. Malay Street. “The Liberator of the Philippines.” The Effect of Climate on Morals. The Charm of the East.

S<sc>ingapore</sc> is interesting geographically, historically, politically, and commercially: but topographically there is little to claim a traveller’s attention. It is an island fourteen miles in its widest part north to south and about twice this width east to west, situated about eighty miles north of the equator off the southernmost point of Asia and separated from the Malay peninsula on the mainland by the Johor (or Johore) Strait, which varies from less than a mile up to two miles wide. The landing wharves are near the southern extremity of the island and the town extends from them in a north-east direction. The conception of its importance, the measures taken to secure it for the Empire, and the purchase of it from the Sultan of Johor were entirely due to the energy and far-sightedness of Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles, whose statue stands in the middle of Raffles Plain on the Esplanade (Padang Besár). Londoners owe a special debt of gratitude to Raffles, who, a year before his death, founded, with Sir Humphry Davy, the “Zoo.” There is a monument to him in Westminster Abbey with the following inscription on the pedestal:—
