The States are particularly interested in Japanese tea, as it takes practically the whole of the tea exported. This is entirely green tea, which is the only kind consumed in the States, made by roasting the leaf over charcoal fires, after a few hours’ drying in the sun. About one-third is “basket-fired” and two-thirds “pan-fired.” The plucking begins in May, and goes on until the end of August. There are usually three pluckings, with a fortnight’s interval between each. Between Nagoya and Shizuoka, where the tea is grown under pear-tree arbours, the first plucking was finished, in 1899, on May 26th. The green tea grown in South Carolina, on the Japanese system, is said to be “as good as any in the world.” China is the only country which competes with Japan in the American tea trade.

We also passed many thick bamboo plantations, and near Nagaike saw acres of pear-trees in full blossom trained on arbours. On our arrival at Nara we went to the Musashino Inn, where I began to use my sheets, which are a luxury unknown to the Japanese, and pillow; and to seek the limited protection afforded by a prodigal use of flea-powder.
