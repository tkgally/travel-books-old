He told me the journey would be very trying and fatiguing; that one must pass through a district of the most poisonous malaria; that when I got there I should be watched and controlled—indeed, that the English Resident was quite a prisoner—and that at that time of year the vast chain of mountains would assuredly be clouded. The Rajah, he said, was then only twelve years of age, and a mere Pagoda. He gave me his photograph, and wrote my name in Sanscrit, which, he said, was the real language of the country; but that the indigenous race who spoke it had been conquered and driven in by the Rajputanas when they fled thither from the tyrannies of the Mogul emperors. Our interview was so pleasant that, before finally leaving Calcutta, I paid him a second visit to say “good-bye.” Singularly enough, a few days after my first visit, I had all his reports confirmed by Mr. Watson, a well-known dentist in Calcutta, to whom I had to appeal for a small timely service to prevent the necessity of a greater one by-and-by. He had been to Katmandoo, in aid of the teeth of the Commander-in-Chief—teeth, you see, can be troublesome in Nepaul—but he would not go again on any account.
