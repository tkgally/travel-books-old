So crowded were the trains on this occasion, that it was rumoured some of the ladies had to be pushed through the windows on returning. For many days after this event the newspapers teemed with accounts of the zealous Volunteers, and of the martial ardour they had betrayed—

<verse>
“To dim their armour’s shine
In glorious battle fray,”
</verse>

appearing almost to regret that the Werribee Plains were not a <i>bonâ fide</i> battle-field. The colonial Volunteers are, indeed, an enthusiastic “institution.” One had only to glance at their ardent countenances to feel assured that an enemy would have no chance in Victoria—to feel that each volunteer would fight, if need be, like the Black Knight of old, as if the strength of twenty men were in his arm,—

<verse>
“And lightning from their eye did part,
Such glance did falcon never dart
When stooping on his prey.”
</verse>
