With the steaming pearly rice and well-seasoned dish of curry, comes a many-partitioned platter containing red and white chutney, both sweet and sour, grated cocoanut, vinegared miniature onions, ground toasted peanuts, minced hard-boiled eggs, pickles of various kinds, and a dozen other delicate condiments and relishes which add zest and seductiveness to the stimulating qualities of this prince of all Oriental bills of fare.

Although less than two years have passed away since my return from the Far East, I have travelled through many lands and partaken of their national dishes. I have feasted to satiety upon the Dutch <i>riz tavel</i> of Java, the popular <i>rassulnick</i> of Russia, the spicy <i>goulasch</i> of Hungary, the far-reaching kraut and Frankfurters of the Fatherland, the savory spaghetti of Italy, the much-beloved <i>pot-à-feu</i> of La Belle France, the muscle-making roast-beef and plum-pudding of Merrie England, the piquant <i>chile con carne</i> of Mexico, the three-finger <i>poi</i> of Hawaii, and the Sunday morning baked-beans and pumpkin-pie of Yankeedom, but among them all I have found nothing to compare, in the tang of its sapidity, to curry and rice, that famous and seductive dish of the Orient.
