At last we reach Nikko. What a scene of beauty and grandeur is before us! A popular Japanese proverb says, “Nikko wo minai uchi wa, ‘kekko’ to iu na!” (“Do not use the word ‘magnificent’ until you have seen Nikko.”) Here nature and art combine to form one of the most wonderful places on the earth. Mountains, cascades, monumental forest trees have always been here. In the seventeenth century, to these were added the mausolea of the illustrious Shogun Ieyasu, founder of the Tokugawa dynasty, and of his scarcely less famous grandson, Iemitsu.

We are surrounded by stately mountains, the greatest of which, the noted Nantai-zan, towers high above its neighbors. Taking jinrikishas, we proceed to the Nikko hotel, a mile and one-half from the station. By this time we are quite accustomed to the Japanese carriage. Though at first one is apt to pity the poor men in their labor of drawing him up and down the hills, in this age of travel, one's nerves and feelings soon become hardened. Reaching the hotel upon the mountain side, some two thousand feet above sea level, we learn, to our disappointment, that it is filled to overflowing.
