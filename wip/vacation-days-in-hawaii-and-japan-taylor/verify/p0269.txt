Both guide and officer are puzzled over this business of “bond clipper,” as in Japan a man is generally an officer, rice-grower, blacksmith, boatman, farmer, artist, carpenter, or has some similar occupation. The officer requests us to call upon his chief, and satisfy him upon this point, so getting into our jinrikishas, we proceed to the chief’s office. I do not get out, and the guide who represents me without doubt has a “hot time,” for when he comes out his face is red. But he laughs and says it is all right. I do not know what he has told the chief, but am convinced that “bond clipping” has been entered upon the Japanese records as a new profession.

While the guide is absent I am much interested in watching the drill of some sixty or seventy school-children, about six or seven years of age. The school-house is a substantial square stone building, standing a hundred feet from the main road. The teacher, a pretty young Japanese girl, is leading them with songs; whether sacred or national airs, I cannot say. They sound to my ears more like a Zulu war-cry.
