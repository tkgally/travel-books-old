Then we ascend to the huge granite <i>torii</i>, and pass a five-storeyed pagoda and some old bronze flower vases, on the way to the Gate of the Two Kings (<i>ni-ō-mon</i>) or front gate (<i>omote-mon</i>), through which we pass into a courtyard. We see that Hidari Jingorō has given the hind legs of his carved elephants hocks instead of knees; and Ieyasu’s tree is pointed out to us. There is also a carving of three monkeys holding their “hands” respectively over the eyes, ears, and mouth to signify, “I see no—I hear no—I speak no—evil.” A fine bronze <i>torii;</i> a water-trough cut out of a block of granite; and an artistically decorated building, containing a complete set of the Buddhist sacred books (<i>kyōmon</i>) are to be seen in this court, and passing up a flight of steps to the next court we see the stone lions presented by Iemitsu; a number of massive seventeenth-century bronze objects, some of which are of European origin; and the temple dedicated to Ieyasu’s patron saint, Yakushi. The interior of this temple, which has a ceiling painted with an enormous dragon by Kano Yasunobu, exhibits beautiful decorations in gold and in colours, and is the most artistic and tasteful in Nikkō. Another flight of steps leads to the finely-carved gate (<i>yōmei-mon</i>), which we pass through to reach the building containing the massive sacred palanquin (<i>mikoshi</i>), and relics connected with Ieyasu.

The Chinese gate (<i>kara-mon</i>), whose pillars are of inlaid Chinese woods, is the last of the series, and leads to the main temple. The oratory of this is decorated with birds and flowers in relief, and contains handsome, large, gold-lacquer boxes and fine embroideries, while the anteroom to the left has some well-executed eagles. Passing at the back of the oratory down three steps, we advance to the chapel, whose six gilt doors bar further progress; but we are permitted to peep through a half-opened one into the dimly-lighted and apparently empty shrine. We retrace our steps, and turning to the left, after passing the Chinese gate, go through the Gate of the Cat (<i>neko-no-mon</i>), over which is the sleeping cat (<i>nemuri-neko</i>) of Hidari Jingorō carved in wood and painted.
