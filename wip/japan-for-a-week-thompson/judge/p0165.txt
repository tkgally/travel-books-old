It may be, it is probable, that the rise of industrialism and Imperialism will gradually kill this innate artistic taste and instinct. Instead of temples the new age is building factories, and the bronze that former generations wrought into majestic triumphs like the Dai-butsu of Kamakura is being flung into the melting-pot to make cannons for the glory of O-mi-kuni.

But for the present the art of Japan survives. It directs the hands of the cunning workers in bronze, enamel, and porcelain at Kyoto. It is manifest in the satin-like smoothness and exquisite joinery of the meanest box in which these artistic products are delivered. It leaps to one’s eyes in town and country, field and park. Japan’s art, together with her huge achievement in war, justify the writers who have called the Japanese “the Greeks of the Far East.”

But even these writers may be surprised to learn that a large and increasing section of modern educated Japanese claim to be Greeks, or descendants of Greeks, in literal fact.
