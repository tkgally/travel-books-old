Standing there by that silent lake, the morning mist enshrouding me, that strange bird in my hand, I fell at once into a train of musing suggested by the thought that this might prove a species new to the world. There is something in such a thought inexpressibly thrilling: to feel that to you alone has been vouchsafed the first glance at a being that has existed for ages undiscovered and unknown; has lived and breathed and sung, generation after generation of the same type; and that you, who now hold its breathless form in your hand, are the first to look upon it! At this age of the world, when man has searched the remotest confines of the globe, to find an animal so high in the scale as this—that has heart and lungs, and in whose veins the blood courses warm and red—is considered an event worthy of chronicling in annals that endure for more than a single generation.

Like these were my reflections that morning,—meditations that caused me to ignore the superstition of my ignorant friend, whose uneasiness regarding the lives of those whom he considered I had placed in jeopardy, was not soon allayed.

Four species of humming-birds greeted me in my first camp in the tropics. They fairly lit up the valley with their gleaming coats; not a bush or tree in flower that did not have one or more hovering above it from morning till night.
