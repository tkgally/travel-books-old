Having been for many months in constant communication with my dusky neighbors, I was no longer looked upon as a foreigner. In fact, it was only when an occasional white man paid a passing visit that I spoke in English at all. I conversed with my own Zanzibaris in their language, the Ki-Swahili, and none of my Lukolela friends knew any tongue but their own Ki-Bangi which I had thoroughly learned.

The study of this revealed to me several abbreviated expressions, scraps of short-hand speech. On the lower reaches of the Congo the word “mboté” at first puzzled my comprehension. If I asked a native to sell me pine-apples or fowls, he answered, “Mboté,” and whether I arrived in the village in the morning, afternoon, or night.
