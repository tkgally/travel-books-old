All that I said to you after our first visit to Kamakura long ago about its extreme desolation and extinction remains, of course, quite true; but one discovers afterwards that, looked at in the light of the nation’s history, there are, perhaps, not many places in Japan which have an interest surpassing that of this vanished city’s site. For Kamakura is closely associated with—owes, in fact, its existence to—that curious tendency in virtue of which the history of Japan differs from that of any other civilised nation (at least so far as I know)—the principle, that is, of a dual government in which all the power was vested in one person and all the dignity in another; in which the functions of government were so remote from the sovereign as to give to European observers the idea of the actual existence of two Emperors in the country—the one “temporal,” as they expressed it, and the other “spiritual.” This principle, which has given so singular a character alike to the institutions and to the history of Japan, may be seen in operation at the very beginnings of the nation’s life; in early times doubtless only in practice, though afterwards also in theory, and its formal repudiation is an event fresh in the memories of people still in middle life.
