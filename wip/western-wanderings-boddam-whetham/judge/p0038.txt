It reminded me of a palace scene in the ‘Arabian Nights.’ The reality, however, was simply a long railway carriage, with chairs like music-stools with arms; some nets, of a size quite incapable of holding anything, and a brilliant array of spittoons. Nor must I forget the filter of water, with one glass or tin cup chained to it, out of which everybody, from the pea-nut boy and porters, was supposed to drink. What would life be to an American without his filter of iced-water!

For the privilege of entering these luxurious structures an extra three dollars per day (if I remember aright) had to be paid. And as the common first-class carriages in America are most uncomfortable, and the second-class quite unfit for anybody to travel in, the consequence is the ‘Pullmans’ are the receptacles for all sorts and conditions of men, and are often crowded in a manner conducive to anything rather than good-temper. In speaking of the cheapness of American railway travelling in comparison with that of other nations, this extra charge for the absolutely necessary convenience of a Pullman car is generally overlooked.
