There is nothing very unusual about these laws. The extraordinary thing <i>is</i> that they are <i>carried out with such absolute strictness and impartiality that natives obtain no liquor.</i> The consequence is that one can go the length and breadth of these islands and never see whisky, brandy, gin, or an intoxicated person. The only case of drunkenness within my knowledge was at an entertainment given to some men-of-war’s men, when liquor was carelessly left “within easy access,” and natives got hold of it. Permits are given to members of the Royal Family, and also, rarely, to high officials and chiefs. As mentioned in Chapter II., even the captain of a steamer is heavily fined if he, in all good faith, gives half a glass of brandy medically to any native on board his ship.

The prohibition law of Fiji is similar to that of Tonga, the penalty for supplying natives with alcoholic liquor being £50 for the first offence, £100 for the second; but the law may at any time become practically inoperative through the following clause, if it be exercised in loose English fashion, and not with the iron strictness of the Tongan minister of police:—

<blockquote>
“The governor may (in case of any person of the native race whom he may deem qualified by character and general sobriety) grant an exemption from the operation of the law.”
</blockquote>

The words in brackets are omitted in the Act of 1892, which repeals former Acts; but the intention is presumably the same as in the former Act. The present governor, with his intimate knowledge of natives, may be competent to exempt; but a new governor, with the ordinary English commercial piety that with a light heart exports to native settlements 10,000 cases of gin, and to clear its conscience adds four missionaries in the same ship,[[fnref 1]] might not.

Exemptions should be strictly limited <i>by law</i> to the great chiefs. In the opinion of some who are well able to judge, it is a mistake, both in Tonga and Fiji, to exempt even them.

How does it happen that efforts to shut out the vampire alcohol can be successful here, and yet have failed so signally elsewhere? Because behind all the liquor laws that can be devised by the wit of man stands, in Tonga and Fiji, a more potent antagonist than even absolute prohibition—the all-powerful, omnipresent, <i>beneficent</i>[[fnref 2]] kava of the Pacific Islands.
