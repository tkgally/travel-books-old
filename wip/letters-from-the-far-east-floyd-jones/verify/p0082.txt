In the far distance the Ghauts Mountains were discernible, whose clay looking sides, enveloped in a slight haze, gave to them a peculiar effect. An immense fleet of merchant vessels of nearly every nationality lay at anchor near us, and the harbor seemed spacious enough to contain a good share of the shipping of the world. Altogether it is one of the most extensive and picturesque harbors I've ever visited.

The Port Surgeon had hardly boarded us before we were surrounded by as motley and varied crafts as one often meets with—from the snug steam launch to the native boat, with its graceful lateen sail and picturesque sailors; conspicuous among which were the Parsees, whose striking black hats at once attract your attention. I could liken it to nothing better than a section of a stovepipe with its upper part cut off at an angle less than forty-five degrees, and with its base set into a sort of cup to fit the head. The Parsee priests wear the same shaped hat, but of white material. We soon passed the inspection of the Customs official, were warmly greeted by some European friends, who were on the lookout for us, and sailed to the landing place at the Apollo Bunder, and, climbing a long flight of stone steps (for the rise and fall of the tide is from 18 to 19 feet), were in India.
