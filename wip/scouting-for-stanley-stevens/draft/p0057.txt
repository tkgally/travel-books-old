Several had deserted with their guns during the night, rascals who had joined the expedition for the advance pay and the chance of making off with a gun, and having touched lip to the hardships of the Nyika, many others in plaintive tones talked of a retreat to Rabai.

At Taro we found, fortunately, plenty of water in several curious round, well-like holes in masses of rock. These rocks are on the top of Taro Hill, and the circular holes, or “ungurunga,” as our Wa-Teita contingent called them, are worthy of mention as curious natural phenomena. The “ungurunga” of Taro Hill are a source of wonderment even to the unreflecting porter. The holes are anywhere from two to four feet in diameter and from four to twelve deep, and such is their uniformity of outline that it seems almost impossible that they should be nature’s sole handiwork. Thomson thinks that both man and nature had a hand in their construction; that water and the chemical action of decayed vegetation first decomposed the coarse sandstone in which they are found and formed small hollows in which water lodged; thirsty men systematically cleared out the sand to make the holes deeper, and thus the process has gone on and on until the little holes have developed into the remarkable reservoirs that now supply whole caravans with abundant water.
