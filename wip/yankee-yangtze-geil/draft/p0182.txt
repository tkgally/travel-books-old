[[cont]]remaining one hundred and twenty-nine li between four <sc>a.m.</sc> and five <sc>p.m.</sc> An amusing incident occurred in the morning. Before retiring, we arranged with the tired fellows to be off at “cock crowing,” for, like many other ancient people, the Chinese take no note of clock time. At three-thirty <sc>a.m.</sc>, when we called them up, they sleepily said, “The cock has not crowed,” and then turned over for another snooze. Because they had not heard chanticleer, therefore he had not crowed. But we persisted in starting, and they finally got up. It was over an hour before a cock actually did crow in our hearing, when we were well on our way on the last spurt for Luchow.

While passing through a narrow, dirty street in the market town of Lishih Chan, that is, “Lift up the Stone,” I saw a man almost naked lying in the street with his face within a few feet of a guard house. He was nearly dead, and had probably been removed from some house in order that he might die in the open. It is bad luck to have a person, especially a stranger, die on a bed. Such a scene as this is witnessed everywhere in China, not only in the country towns, but also in rich cities like Chungking. After being ferried across the Yangtze, which we reached at this point, we came into Luchow, and found our way to the house of some Australian missionaries, who gave me a hearty welcome.
