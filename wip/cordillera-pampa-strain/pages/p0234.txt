However, we arrived at Coro Corto, the end of the stage, about 3 <sc>p.m.</sc>, finding the latter part of the route more thickly inhabited and better watered. As we had journeyed about ninety-six miles, according to the computations generally received, we determined to tarry for the night, especially as the next post house held out, according to Don Antonio, but few inducements in the way of food and lodging for a night’s stay, and was, moreover, liable to an inroad of the Pampa Indians, who were, at the time, ravaging some settlements a few days’ journey in advance. The post of Coro Corto consisted of three houses, built on three sides of a square of the invariable <i>adobes</i>, and one story in height. The floors were clay, but there was an appearance of cleanliness about the premises, which augured favourably for our supper, in which we were not disappointed; as at dark we sat down to a nicely cooked <i>casuela</i>, and enjoyed, moreover, the somewhat unusual luxury of a dish, spoon, knife and fork, for each individual present. After supper, we made our beds in the cool air of the court yard, using our saddles for pillows, and by the time we finished our cigar, were perfectly prepared to consign ourselves to the rest so essential after a hard day’s journey, with a more tedious one in perspective for the morrow.
