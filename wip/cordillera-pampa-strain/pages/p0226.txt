But as our friend Don Walter, the author of “Deck and Port,” frequently inquires after an episode, “What has all this to do with doubling Cape Horn?” what has this to do with crossing the pampas? The echo answers nothing. So to use a time-honoured quotation which has perhaps appeared at least once in every popular book of travels during the present century, “<i>[[corr Revenous|Revenons]] à nous mouton,</i>” which in my particular instance will translate—Leaving philanthropists, grizzlies and alligators, as also the Fejees and negroes whom the advocates of the unity would fain libel us by saddling upon the high caste Caucasian, we will return to our horse.

A few hours’ ride and Don Antonio and myself were sworn friends, a desirable state of things, to which my gift of a poncho before starting, and my two thick blankets at our journey’s end in perspective, I have no doubt contributed, though I am willing to do justice to his many generous and noble traits, which I had frequently occasion to admire during my journey. During the morning he dropped alongside me and defined our respective positions, which was perfectly satisfactory to both parties. He supported his own dignity as a government officer by declining to call me <i>patron</i>, master, or employer, but would call me <i>compañero</i> or companion. I was, however, to have, under all circumstances, the second best horse, the first cut of the <i>roast</i>, the first drink at the <i>chifre</i>, and was not to have the trouble of saddling or bridling my own animal, which was to be done as an understood favour by either the postilion, the courier, or by one of his numerous <i>compadres</i> whom we afterward encountered at every post-house.
