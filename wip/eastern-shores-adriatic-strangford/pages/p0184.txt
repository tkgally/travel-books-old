[[cont]]the three delightful days we had spent in Tsetinje. Just before supper, I was told that Mirko had been hovering about my door, wanting to show me something. I went out at once, and he said, ‘You must not leave our mountain without seeing our best and most interesting possession.’ So he led me to the end of the corridor, where a glass-case was arranged, protecting two or three hundred medals of gold and silver, taken from the breasts of the Turks in last year’s war. Scores of them were English and French Crimean medals. A dozen soiled and battered Turkish banners stood beside them, and along the wall hung a very large number of scymetars, daggers, &c., taken at the same time. It was natural that Mirko should show me these—not the Prince: Mirko glories in the war—the Prince sorrows for it. A Turkish Pasha had come to Tsetinje to settle some business with the Prince a week or two before my visit; but when I alluded to the medals, &c., the Prince said, eagerly, ‘Oh! I would not be guilty of such a rudeness as that! I carried them all away before he came, and shut them up in my own bedroom.’
