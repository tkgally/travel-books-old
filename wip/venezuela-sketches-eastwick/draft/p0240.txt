At 8.30 we reached Las Guias, a neat village four and a half miles from Valencia, and about as far from the lake, and in an hour more we were at Guicara, and at 10 <sc>a.m</sc>, at San Joaquin, distant respectively eleven and seventeen miles from Valencia, and both of them villages on the northern coast of the lake. Before noon, still travelling in the same direction, we reached the coffee and cotton plantation of the Señores Gascue and Careño. Here we alighted to breakfast. The situation of the house is most picturesque. It abuts on a hill, 200 feet high, densely clothed with shrubs. Beyond this was a belt of wood, and then a line of glorious mountains. Panthers, they said, were numerous in these mountains, and they pointed out a spot where a very large one was known to have his den. He had done a great deal of mischief, but no one was bold enough to attack him in his steep retreat. An aqueduct had been built to the side of the nearest mountain, and along this a fine stream of water was leaping in mid-air, foaming and glittering. It fell close to the mill where the coffee was being husked, and they said it was usual for travellers to take a bathe in it. However, not wishing to get a chill, and, perhaps, fever, I declined the experiment.

Before we went to breakfast another guest arrived, a Don Realte, who, it appeared, was the owner of a neighbouring plantation. He told us that the committee for the enlistment of soldiers was sitting at no great distance, and that a detachment of 160 men had been sent to him to obtain recruits among his labourers.
